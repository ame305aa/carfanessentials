Full history

From the first cut to the closed box.

Carfan exists so frankincense from this region can be finished here. The resin does not leave as an unnamed sack. It becomes oil, cream, soap, and shampoo, packed in Jigjiga with the tree still visible on the label.

Why the work sits in Jigjiga

Jigjiga is the city of the Somali Region, on the edge of the dry country where frankincense trees have been known for generations. The groves are not in the middle of town. They are out where the rock is hot and the trees are spaced far apart. What belongs in the city is the finishing: the sorting table, the still, the bench, and the people who pack.

Carfan is that finishing. We are an essentials workshop. The mark on the door is the same mark on the bottle — a frankincense tree, drawn spare, held in a circle. The circle is deliberate. A house does not need only an oil. It needs something to wash with, something to smooth on skin, something for the hair. One ingredient, four families.

The colors come from the resin itself. Resin brown is the tear when it deepens. Bark brown is the tree. Gold is the pale tear, still fresh. Those three colors are the only colors on the pack.

Sourcing to sending

The seven steps we actually run.

This is the path of a batch. Nothing on this list is skipped to make a season shorter.

01

Sourcing — before anyone cuts

A season starts with a conversation, not a knife. Carfan sits with harvester families and agrees which groves will be visited, which trees will be left alone, and what a kilogram of tears will be paid. The price is set before the first cut, so a family can see the season clearly.

We buy resin as resin from the people who tapped it. A lot is named by the grove it came from. If a sack cannot be named, it does not come into the workshop.

02

Tapping — a shallow cut, then waiting

The cut is shallow. It opens the bark enough for the tree to weep a tear of frankincense, and no deeper. Harvesters return over the following weeks and lift the tears by hand. A tree that has given is rested before it is asked again. Deep cuts and hurried second tappings are refused, even when a season is thin.

Young tappers learn this beside someone who already knows the trees: where to cut, when to stop, and how to leave the tree able to give another year.

03

Carrying — shade, cleanliness, the road to Jigjiga

Fresh tears are sticky. They are dried in the shade, kept off the ground, and cleaned of bark, dust, and leaf before they travel. What arrives at the workshop should smell of resin, not of the sack.

The road ends in Jigjiga. From this point the batch does not leave the city until it is a finished essential.

04

Sorting — one table, several grades

On the table the tears are separated by color, size, and scent. Pale gold, clean tears are kept for the still. Larger, deeper pieces are set aside for the infusions that go into cream, soap, and shampoo. Dust and bark that escaped the grove are picked out.

Sorting is paid work in the workshop, held by neighbors. A grade is written down before anything is heated. We do not blend first and name it later.

05

Distilling and infusing — the still and the warm pan

Pale tears meet steam. The oil separates from the water and is rested in glass until it clears and the scent settles. That resting is part of the making. Oil that is bottled the hour it leaves the still is not a Carfan oil.

The deeper grades take a different path. They are warmed slowly into oils that will be blended into the face cream, the body cream, the soap, and the shampoo. This is how one tree reaches every family of the range. Lemongrass, when it is in the plan, is distilled on its own and kept as its own oil — the bright bottle in the brand book.

06

Making — small batches, one book

The bench is where the essentials become themselves. Creams are blended and filled into jars while they are still even. Soaps are poured, cut, and left to cure before a belly band goes on. Shampoo is mixed, checked for feel, and bottled. Each batch is named and dated in the workshop book: grove, grade, day, and who filled it.

Batches stay small on purpose. A problem can be seen. A neighbor can learn the pour. Women from Jigjiga who want the craft can take a turn at the soap bench and carry the skill home.

07

Packing and leaving — the last mark is the tree

A finished essential gets the Carfan pack: a white ground, a pale branch behind the name, a spine in resin, gold, or bark, and the tree inside its circle. Oil uses the resin spine. Cream uses gold. Soap uses bark. Shampoo carries the resin pump. The small line on the label reads that it was made in Jigjiga.

Boxes are closed in the same room they were filled. From here they go to homes and to shops, or they wait for someone who calls the workshop. If you still have a Carfan oil bottle, bring it back. When that oil is in the still again, we refill it.

That is the end of the path: a thing you can use, a pack you can read, and a bottle that can come home to the bench.

What happens next

The pack is the public face of this path.

Every bottle, jar, bar, and wash on the next page is the end of these seven steps. The circle on the label is the grove, the table, the still, and the bench, kept together.
